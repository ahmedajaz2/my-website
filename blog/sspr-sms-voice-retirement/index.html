📱 SMS and Voice Are Leaving Password Reset Too

🗓️ Published: November 2026 | ✍️ Author: Ajaz Ahmed

When Microsoft announced it was retiring its own SMS and voice MFA services, most of the attention went to sign-in. Fair enough - that is the part users feel every day. But the same retirement hits self-service password reset (SSPR) too, and the timeline works differently than most people assume. Microsoft's MC1536049 gives you a 50-day transition for password reset after the sign-in retirement reaches your tenant. Here is how the clock actually runs, the trap passkey users fall into, and what to do before it runs out.

⏱️ The Timeline Is Not One Date

The sign-in retirement starts February 1, 2027 for most users, and July 1, 2027 for Global Admins and external users. But those are phased rollout start dates, not a single cutoff. Your 50-day SSPR transition begins when the applicable rollout reaches your tenant - so if the rollout reaches you on February 1, your SSPR transition ends around March 22. If it reaches you later, your clock starts later. Do not plan around a fixed March 22 date; plan around the rollout hitting your tenant.

Take: the date that matters is not February 1 - it is the day the rollout lands in your tenant, plus 50 days.

🔄 What Happens During the 50 Days

During the transition, existing SMS and voice registrations keep working for password reset under your current recovery policy - so nothing breaks on day one. What stops immediately is registration: SMS/voice SSPR registration and refresh prompts stop, and users can no longer add SMS/voice methods or edit authentication phone numbers in My Sign-Ins. Confusingly, existing phone registrations appear disabled in My Sign-Ins even while they still work for password reset during the transition. Expect helpdesk calls about that one.

Take: the 50 days are a grace period for recovery, not a window to onboard anyone new onto SMS/voice. Nobody new can register.

🚫 What Happens After the 50 Days

Once the transition ends, Microsoft-provided SMS and voice are no longer SSPR recovery options at all. Users with enough other eligible methods can still reset their password with those. Users without enough cannot complete SSPR and are directed to contact an administrator - which is a helpdesk ticket, not a self-service flow. The one/two method requirement still applies, so "enough" means whatever your SSPR policy demands.

Take: after the transition, password reset for affected users becomes a manual process unless you prepared an alternative.

🪪 The Passkey Trap

Here is the part that catches teams off guard: passkeys and other sign-in methods do not automatically satisfy SSPR requirements. A user can sign in happily with a passkey and still be stuck when they forget their password, because password reset needs its own eligible recovery methods. This affects users who have another sign-in method but still depend on SMS/voice for password recovery - which is a larger group than most teams expect. Pushing passkeys is the right move for sign-in, but it does not close the password reset gap on its own.

Take: audit recovery methods separately from sign-in methods. A passkey user with SMS as their only recovery method is still at risk.

🎯 What to Do Now

Start with the same inventory you would run for the sign-in retirement, but ask a different question: who relies on SMS/voice for password recovery, even if they have another sign-in method? Then make sure those users have enough eligible methods to meet your SSPR policy without Microsoft SMS/voice - Authenticator app notification or code, a second email, security questions where allowed. If your organization needs phone-based recovery for regulatory or operational reasons, configure and test a supported customer-managed telephony provider (CYOT) before the transition ends - recovery through a supported provider stays available, only Microsoft-sent messages retire. Then test password recovery against your SSPR policies and Conditional Access settings, and tell users and the helpdesk what is changing.

One more option worth weighing: the "Allow password change after secure sign-in" setting (MC1437671) lets passkey users change a forgotten password from My Sign-Ins after a secure sign-in, without knowing their current password. It is off by default, needs admin enablement, and the toggle is tenant-wide with no per-user or per-group scoping - and Microsoft will not enable it for you automatically. For a passkey-heavy population, it is a useful fallback.

💬 Closing Thoughts

The sign-in retirement got the headlines, but password reset is where the disruption lands for users who forget a password on a bad day. The 50-day transition is generous if you use it, and useless if you discover it after it ends. Identify who depends on SMS/voice for recovery, give them another way to reset, and do not assume a passkey covers it.

  • ✅ Identify users relying on SMS/voice for SSPR - including those with another sign-in method.
  • ✅ Ensure enough eligible recovery methods to meet your SSPR policy without Microsoft SMS/voice.
  • ✅ Configure and test a CYOT provider if phone-based recovery is needed for regulatory or operational reasons.
  • ✅ Evaluate the "Allow password change after secure sign-in" toggle (MC1437671) as a fallback for passkey users.
  • ✅ Test password recovery against SSPR policies and Conditional Access, then brief users and the helpdesk.
